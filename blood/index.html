Two brothers in a field. A question God asks. A voice that comes from the ground.

I

The ledger

TPoT The song is sung by someone who envies a brother, keeps score and lets the call ring. Its second verse names where that road first ran: “Am I my brother’s keeper / That’s the question Cain let hang.”

Genesis 4 begins with two offerings:

And in process of time it came to pass, that Cain brought of the fruit of the ground an offering unto the LORD. And Abel, he also brought of the firstlings of his flock and of the fat thereof. And the LORD had respect unto Abel and to his offering: But unto Cain and to his offering he had not respect. And Cain was very wroth, and his countenance fell.

Genesis 4:3–5 · KJV

Genesis doesn’t say why one offering was received and the other wasn’t. See “Why Abel’s offering?” below.

II

Who speaks in Genesis 4?

Read the whole story once. Then choose a person and see which words in it are theirs.

Genesis records no spoken words from Abel

He keeps sheep, brings an offering and goes out to the field. Genesis says Cain “talked with” him, but gives no words from either brother. The only voice Abel has in the story is his blood, and it is God who hears it.

…the voice of thy brother’s blood crieth unto me from the ground.

Genesis 4:10 · KJV

The New Testament doesn’t let that voice go quiet. Jesus names him “righteous Abel” (Matthew 23:35), and Hebrews says:

By faith Abel offered unto God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain, by which he obtained witness that he was righteous, God testifying of his gifts: and by it he being dead yet speaketh.

Hebrews 11:4 · KJV

III

Better things than that of Abel

The song’s turn comes from one verse in Hebrews. The writer reminds believers what they have come to:

And to Jesus the mediator of the new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling, that speaketh better things than that of Abel.

Hebrews 12:24 · KJV

Abel’s blood cried out about a wrong done. Hebrews says the blood of Jesus speaks “better things”. The song hears that as mercy.

John Chrysostom · Homilies on Hebrews, Homily 32, §2

“Did then the [blood] of Abel speak? Yea, he says, and by it he being dead yet speaks.”

Chrysostom (d. 407), preaching verse by verse through Hebrews, links Hebrews 12:24 back to Hebrews 11:4 and Genesis 4:10. He says Christ’s blood speaks “more clear and more distinct” because it “has cleansed all men”. He is explaining the contrast in Hebrews, not claiming that Abel’s cry was wrong.

Read deeper: what God says before and after

God speaks to Cain before anything happens, when his face has fallen:

If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.

Genesis 4:7 · KJV

Augustine of Hippo · City of God, Book XV, chapter 7

“Did he not fulfill his wicked intention of killing his brother even after he was warned by God’s voice?”

Augustine, writing in the early fifth century, dwells on the warning in verse 7: God spoke, and Cain went ahead. Note that Augustine read Genesis in a Latin translation, so his wording of verse 7 differs from the Hebrew text behind the KJV (“If you offer rightly, but do not rightly distinguish, have you not sinned?”).

Read City of God, Book XV (chapter 7)

After the killing God still asks a question first, “Where is Abel thy brother?”, and when Cain fears that anyone who finds him will kill him, God protects him from revenge (4:15). Readers have long asked what the mark was. Genesis doesn’t describe it; it only says what it was for: “lest any finding him should kill him.”

Read deeper: why Abel’s offering?

Genesis doesn’t say. Readers have answered from different texts, and they don’t all agree:

  • Hebrews 11:4 says Abel offered “by faith”.
  • 1 John 3:12 points to how each brother lived: “his own works were evil, and his brother’s righteous.”
  • Some readers point to the details in Genesis 4:4, where Abel brings “the firstlings of his flock and of the fat thereof”, the first and best. Genesis itself doesn’t draw that conclusion.

Irenaeus of Lyons · Against Heresies, Book III, 23.4

“…with envy and malice thought that he could domineer over him…”

Writing in the late second century, Irenaeus says Cain was “counselled by God to keep quiet” and did not listen. He sees Cain’s answer, “Am I my brother’s keeper?”, as “extending and aggravating” the wrong. Irenaeus wrote in Greek and, like Augustine, read verse 7 in a translation whose wording differs from the KJV’s; his “equitable division” follows that wording.

Read Against Heresies III.23

These are three readers, not a single verdict of the early Church.

IV

If you were the one harmed

Abel is not a lesson in this story. He is a brother who was killed. God doesn’t ask his blood to be quiet; God hears it.

TPoT The song is sung by someone who did the wrong and is learning to reach out. If you are the one who was hurt, nothing here asks you to go back to someone who isn’t safe, or to pretend the harm was small. If you are in danger, getting safe comes first.

If you want to pray

Lord Jesus Christ, You heard Abel’s blood from the ground, and Your own blood speaks better things. Where I keep a ledger, teach me mercy. Where I have been wronged, hear me. Keep my hands from closing when my brother reaches out, and give me wisdom to know how to answer. Amen.
