2 Samuel 11–12 · Psalm 51

Scripture · KJV

But David tarried still at Jerusalem. And it came to pass in an eveningtide, that David arose from off his bed, and walked upon the roof of the king’s house

2 Samuel 11:1–2 · KJV

And it came to pass in the morning, that David wrote a letter to Joab, and sent it by the hand of Uriah. … Set ye Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle, and retire ye from him, that he may be smitten, and die.

2 Samuel 11:14–15 · KJV

Uriah carried his own death order. When Bathsheba heard that her husband was dead, she mourned for him (2 Samuel 11:26). Their names stay in this story.

Nathan’s story

Retold from 2 Samuel 12:1–4

Two men lived in one city. The rich man had many flocks and herds. The poor man had one little ewe lamb. It grew up with his children, ate from his plate and slept in his arms. When a traveller came, the rich man spared his own flock and took the poor man’s lamb.

You are the judge. What should happen to the rich man?

David gave his verdict

And David’s anger was greatly kindled against the man; and he said to Nathan, As the LORD liveth, the man that hath done this thing shall surely die: And he shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity.

2 Samuel 12:5–6 · KJV

“Thou art the man.”

2 Samuel 12:7 · KJV

And David said unto Nathan, I have sinned against the LORD. And Nathan said unto David, The LORD also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die.

2 Samuel 12:13 · KJV

David is forgiven, and Uriah is still dead. Mercy does not undo what was done. Psalm 51 is the prayer of someone who has stopped defending himself:

Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me.

Psalm 51:10 · KJV

Where is it easier to judge someone else than to tell the truth about yourself?

Read with the early Church

Commentary, not Scripture

Clement of Rome · First Letter to the Corinthians, chapters 18–19

Writing to a church divided by conflict, Clement calls David an example of humility, quotes Psalm 51 at length, and then asks the Corinthians to seek peace. David’s prayer becomes a whole community’s way back.

Read chapters 18–19 ↗

Augustine of Hippo · Exposition on Psalm 51, §§2–3

“For many men will to fall with David, and will not to rise with David.”

Augustine warns against using David’s story as permission to fall, and speaks to the one who has already fallen: you can still get up.

Read §§2–3 ↗

John Chrysostom · Homily 4 on Second Corinthians, §§6–7

Chrysostom points to a later scene: when Shimei curses David, the king refuses to let his men kill him (2 Samuel 16:5–13). He reads that restraint as the sign of a humbled heart.

Read §§6–7 ↗
